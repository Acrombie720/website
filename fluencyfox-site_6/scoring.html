How Fluencyfox scores

Scoring methodology  ·  Last updated: 02/10/2026

Fluencyfox scores how well someone does real work with AI, from evidence of them doing it, against a published AI fluency framework. Every score comes from what a candidate produced and how they got there, never from self-report or recall.

Each assessment produces three section scores and an overall score out of 10, with written reasons and links to the evidence behind every point.

1. What a candidate does

Candidates work on a realistic task set by the employer, in the time the employer sets, with whatever AI tools they would normally use in the job. Alongside them is Maya, an AI stakeholder who knows the brief and the business context behind it, and answers questions the way a manager would.

Four sources of evidence come out of every session:

SourceWhat it shows
Submitted workThe deliverable itself: documents, code, spreadsheets, analysis
Maya conversationEvery question the candidate asked, and what they did with the answers
Spoken narrationThe candidate’s reasoning, transcribed with timestamps
Screen analysisThe prompts candidates type into their own AI tools, the replies, and how those replies reach the work

Screen analysis is what lets us score how candidates use their own tools, such as ChatGPT, Claude or Copilot. It describes the work on screen, never the person: it does not read faces, expressions or attention.

2. The three scored sections

Every assessment scores three sections from 0 to 10, each built from sub-skills scored 0 to 2. The employer sets each section’s weight per test.

SectionDefault weightSub-skills
Output Quality50%Task completion, technical accuracy, presentation and clarity, documentation, originality
Communication30%Clarity, technical knowledge, logical reasoning, completeness of the story they tell
AI Collaboration20%Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence

Two rules stop good talk from covering for weak work:

  • Talk is not a deliverable. A candidate whose Output Quality is 5 or below cannot score above 6 overall, however well they communicated.
  • Missing or off-task work scores 0. A polished answer to a different question earns nothing.

3. Built on Anthropic’s AI Fluency Framework

Our AI Collaboration score uses the four competencies of the AI Fluency Framework as its sub-skills, by name. The framework was published in 2025 by Prof Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design) and Prof Joseph Feller (University College Cork), developed with Anthropic and supported by Ireland’s Higher Education Authority. It is openly licensed and already underpins university courses in the US and Ireland.

CompetencyThe framework asksWhat we score
DelegationWhich parts of the work to give AI, and which to keepWhether open questions went to the right place before the work rested on them, and whether AI got bounded jobs it does well
DescriptionTelling the AI what it needsWhether prompts carry the candidate’s own context, constraints and goal, rather than a pasted brief
DiscernmentJudging what comes backWhether the candidate checked, challenged or corrected AI output before relying on it
DiligenceOwning the resultWhether what reaches the deliverable is the candidate’s own, fits the brief and carries no unreviewed AI text

Each competency has written anchors from 0 to 2, so the same score means the same behaviour across every submission. The framework’s authors describe the competencies; the anchors, and how evidence counts toward them, are our rules.

5. How a score is produced

Scores come from an AI judge that investigates the evidence before it scores, and then has to show its working. It reads every source in full, and it can run the candidate’s submitted files and code in an isolated environment with no network access, so a claim like “the analysis is correct” is checked by running it rather than by reading it.

Our server checks every citation the judge gives against the evidence it names, and a verdict that fails is sent back to be fixed. The judge only scores sub-skills. Our own code turns those into section and overall scores, so the employer’s weights and the caps are applied exactly the same way every time.

6. Safeguards

  • Every point is cited. Each strength, and every sub-score of 2 or 0, names the file, the prompt or the moment in the recording behind it. In the report those are links, so a reviewer can check any claim in one click.
  • The maths is code, not AI. Section scores, weights, caps and the overall score are calculated by our code, so they cannot drift.
  • The employer adds, never overrides. Employers describe what good looks like for the role and weight the three sections. Those notes sharpen the scoring but cannot change the rubric, the caps or the scale.
  • We assess the work, never the person. Nothing reads faces, emotion, attention or demeanour, in line with the EU AI Act’s ban on emotion recognition in recruitment. The webcam is only used to check that someone is in frame, and that never moves a score.
  • People decide. The score informs a hiring decision; it does not make one. The report shows the reasons so a recruiter can agree, disagree and record their own rating.

How we handle assessment data is set out in our privacy policy.

7. Setting a minimum AI level

Because every test uses the same anchors, a score is a level you can set a bar against, for new hires and current staff alike.

Overall scoreWhat it means
9 to 10Exceptional: exceeds the brief across the board. Rare.
8Excellent: clearly exceeds the baseline
6 to 7Meets expectations: delivers the task end to end. The target for a solid hire.
4 to 5Partially meets: engaged seriously but fell short of the deliverable
0 to 3Below expectations

We suggest setting a minimum on two numbers: 6 overall (can they deliver with AI), plus a separate floor on AI Collaboration (do they work with AI well), tuned to how central AI is to the role. Running the same test with current staff first gives you an internal benchmark before the bar is fixed.
